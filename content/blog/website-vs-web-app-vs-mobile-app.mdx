A founder walks into a meeting and says, "We need an app." Often what they actually need is a good website and a WhatsApp number. Sometimes they need a web app. Occasionally, they really do need something in the Play Store.

Getting this wrong is expensive. Build an app too early and you spend months and lakhs on something nobody downloads. Build only a website when customers need a daily tool, and you lose them to a competitor who made it easy.

This post explains the three options in plain English, gives you a decision table by goal, covers the PWA middle path, and ends with a checklist and a step list for scoping your first version.

<KeyTakeaways>
- A website helps people find and trust you; an app helps them do something repeatedly.
- Most startups should start with a website, then add a web app or PWA when usage is proven.
- Native mobile apps make sense for daily use, offline needs or deep phone features.
- No-code and AI builders are great for testing ideas; graduate to custom code when limits bite.
</KeyTakeaways>

## Plain-English definitions

### Website

Pages people read. Your home page, services, pricing, about, contact. Visitors don't log in; they learn about you and then call, fill a form or message you on WhatsApp. A coaching institute's site listing batches and fees is a website.

### Web app

Software that runs in the browser. Users log in and do things: book a slot, track an order, upload documents, see a dashboard. Nothing to install, works on laptop and phone, and when you update it, everyone gets the new version immediately. A clinic's patient portal is a web app.

### Mobile app

Software downloaded from the Google Play Store or Apple App Store. It lives on the home screen, can send push notifications reliably, and can use the camera, GPS, Bluetooth and offline storage more deeply. A food-delivery app is a mobile app.

The lines blur, and that's fine. What matters is what your customer needs to do.

## Decision table: start from the goal

| Your main goal | What usually fits first | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Get found on Google and look credible | Website | Search engines index pages; nobody downloads an app to check you out |
| Convert enquiries into leads | Website with forms and WhatsApp | Fast pages and one-tap contact beat an install step |
| Deliver a service online (bookings, portals, dashboards) | Web app | Logins and workflows without asking people to install anything |
| Repeat daily use (ordering, tracking, habit products) | Mobile app, or a PWA to start | Home-screen presence and notifications drive return visits |
| Offline use or deep device features (GPS tracking, Bluetooth, camera-heavy) | Mobile app | Browsers still limit some device access |

Notice that two of the five goals are solved by a website. For most Indian SMBs, such as a real-estate firm, a restaurant or a D2C brand in its first year, the website is the product's front door, and that's where the first rupee should go. ## The PWA middle path

A progressive web app (PWA) is a web app that behaves a bit like a mobile app. Users can add it to their home screen, it opens full-screen, loads quickly, and can work partly offline.

For many startups this is the sensible bridge. You build once, it works on Android and iPhone, you skip app store review, and you can ship fixes the same day. If usage grows and you need deeper phone features, you can still build native apps later, with real data on what people use.

The trade-offs are honest ones. PWAs don't show up in app store search, push notifications have historically been less reliable on iPhone than Android, and some hardware features remain out of reach. If your customers expect to find you in the Play Store, a PWA alone may feel incomplete.

<Callout type="tip" title="Test demand before the app stores">If you're unsure whether people will come back daily, launch as a web app or PWA first. Daily active use is the signal that justifies a native app.</Callout>

## Cost and time, at a general level

Without a scope, any specific number is a guess. But the relative order is reliable.

- **Website:** the cheapest and fastest. Most small sites take a few weeks.
- **Web app:** more expensive, because you're building logins, data, permissions and workflows. Usually a couple of months for a focused first version.
- **Mobile app:** often the most expensive, especially if you need both Android and iOS, plus a backend and an admin panel. Cross-platform frameworks reduce this, but testing on many devices and store submissions add time.

Ongoing costs follow the same order. An app needs hosting, monitoring, bug fixes, OS updates and store compliance every year. Budget for that before you budget for the launch.

## App store realities

Founders often picture the Play Store as a free distribution channel. It isn't quite that.

- **Review takes time.** Both stores review apps before they go live, and updates go through review too. Apple's process is generally stricter. A rejection can delay a launch by days.
- **Updates aren't instant.** Even after approval, many users don't update promptly. You'll support older versions for a while.
- **Discoverability is hard.** Millions of apps compete for attention. Most installs come from your own marketing, such as Instagram, ads, referrals and your website, not from store search.
- **Policies change.** Payments, data collection and permissions rules are updated regularly. Check the current guidelines of each store before you design features around them.
- **Data protection.** If you collect personal data from Indian users, the DPDP Act applies at a general level. Get advice on consent and data handling early, not after launch.

<Callout type="note" title="Your website still matters">Even with an app, people will Google you before installing. A clear website with store links does a lot of the persuading.</Callout>

## No-code and AI builders: great for prototypes

You can now get a working prototype without a development team. These tools are genuinely useful at the idea stage:

- **Lovable, Bolt.new and v0** turn plain-language descriptions into working web interfaces and code. Good for clickable prototypes and simple internal tools.
- **Bubble** builds full web apps with logins, databases and workflows visually.
- **Glide** turns a spreadsheet into a simple app, handy for internal tools like field-staff checklists.
- **FlutterFlow** builds mobile apps visually, with the option to export code.

Use them to test whether anyone wants the thing. Show the prototype to ten customers, watch where they get confused, and iterate. You can browse more options in our [AI app builders directory](/ai-tools/apps).

### When to graduate to custom code

Move on when you hit one of these:

- Performance slows as users or data grow.
- You need integrations the tool can't handle cleanly, like a specific payment flow, ERP sync or WhatsApp API.
- Platform costs rise steeply with usage.
- You need control over security, data location or compliance.
- Investors or enterprise customers ask about your tech stack and ownership.

None of this means the prototype was wasted. It's the best spec you'll ever write.

## Five questions to decide

Answer these honestly before talking to any developer.

1. **What must the customer do?** Read and contact you (website), log in and complete a task (web app), or use it daily on the go (mobile app)?
2. **How often will they come back?** Once a month is a website or web app. Several times a week starts to justify an app.
3. **Do you need phone features?** Background GPS, Bluetooth, offline-first or heavy camera use point towards native.
4. **How will people find you?** If through Google, start with a website. If through the Play Store, plan for a store listing.
5. **What can you afford to run for a year,** not just to build? Include hosting, fixes, updates and marketing.

## Scoping your MVP, step by step

An MVP is the smallest version that proves people will use and pay for your product. Here's how to scope one.

1. **Write the problem in one sentence,** in your customer's words.
2. **Pick one type of user** to serve first, such as patients, not patients plus doctors plus admins.
3. **Map the core journey** from first visit to the moment they get value, in five to eight steps.
4. **Cut every feature** that isn't on that journey. Ratings, referrals and dark mode can wait.
5. **Decide the platform** using the decision table and the five questions above.
6. **Prototype it** with a no-code or AI builder, or on paper, and test with real customers.
7. **List integrations** you truly need on day one, such as Razorpay, WhatsApp or SMS.
8. **Define success** for the first 60 days: a number you'll track, like weekly bookings.
9. **Get a fixed-scope quote** for that version only, with a clear change process.
