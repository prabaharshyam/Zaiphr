You've seen a competitor's product pop up in five different reels in a week, and your DMs are full of creators asking to "collab". You know influencer marketing works for some brands. You also know a few people who sent out ₹50,000 of free product and got nothing back but a story that disappeared in 24 hours.

The difference is rarely luck. It is picking the right size of creator, checking them properly, giving them a brief that lets them sound like themselves, and measuring sales instead of likes.

This guide walks through that process step by step, with a creator-tier table, a vetting checklist and a simple way to work out what each customer actually cost you.

<KeyTakeaways>
- For small budgets, nano and micro creators usually give you more tests per rupee than one big name.
- Vet engagement quality and audience location, not follower count.
- Brief the goal and the must-says, then let the creator write it their way.
- Put disclosure, usage rights and payment terms in writing, even for barter.
- Judge every creator on cost per acquisition, tracked with unique codes and UTM links.

## Nano, micro, macro: what the tiers mean

Creator tiers are industry shorthand, not official categories. Different agencies draw the lines in different places, but the rough ranges below are common.

| Tier | Typical follower range | What they're good for | Typical deal shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nano | About 1,000–10,000 | Local trust, honest reviews, tight communities | Barter or small fee |
| Micro | About 10,000–1,00,000 | Niche authority, steady engagement, content you can reuse | Paid, sometimes barter plus fee |
| Mid-tier | About 1,00,000–5,00,000 | Wider reach inside a niche | Paid, often via a manager |
| Macro | About 5,00,000–10,00,000 | Broad awareness, launches | Paid, agency or manager involved |
| Mega / celebrity | 10,00,000+ | Mass reach, brand prestige | Large fees, formal contracts |

Treat these as a starting point. A home baker in Indore with 6,000 followers who all live within 10 km may be worth more to a local cake shop than a Mumbai lifestyle creator with 3 lakh.

## Why smaller creators often fit small budgets

The maths is simple. Say you have ₹1,00,000 for a first campaign. That could be one post from a bigger creator, or 10–20 posts from nano and micro creators in your niche.

With one creator, you learn whether that one person worked. With fifteen, you learn which niches, formats and messages work, and you end up with fifteen pieces of content you can reuse.

Smaller creators also tend to answer comments themselves, so a follower's "Is this good for oily skin?" gets a reply from someone they trust.

Bigger creators make sense once you know your message works and want reach fast, say ahead of a Diwali sale.

## Step 1: Find the right creators

You don't need a paid discovery platform to start. Spend a few evenings on these four sources.

### Search and hashtags

Search Instagram and YouTube for your category plus your city or language: "skincare Tamil", "Pune food", "home workouts Hindi". Browse niche hashtags and note who keeps appearing with real conversation in the comments, not just emoji.

### Your own customers

Look through your tagged posts, Google reviews and WhatsApp chats. Customers who already post about you are the easiest yes and the most believable voice. A loyal customer with 3,000 engaged followers is a better first partner than a stranger with 30,000.

### Regional language creators

A large share of India consumes content in Hindi, Tamil, Telugu, Marathi, Bengali, Kannada and other languages. Regional creators often have very loyal audiences and less brand competition for their time. If your customers speak Tamil at home, a Tamil creator explaining your product will usually feel more natural than an English one.

### Shortlist in a sheet

Track candidates in a sheet: handle, niche, followers, average reel views, language, city and contact. Aim for 30–40 names so you still have 10–15 after vetting.

<Callout type="tip" title="Start with people who already like you">Search your tagged posts and reviews before you search strangers. Existing customers say yes faster and sound more convincing on camera.</Callout>

## Step 2: Vet every creator before you pay

This is where most small brands skip ahead and lose money. Ten minutes per profile saves a lot of regret.

### Vetting checklist

1. **Engagement quality.** Read the comments. Are people asking real questions and tagging friends, or is it "Nice!!" and fire emojis from random accounts?
2. **Views versus followers.** Look at average views on the last 10 reels. If a 50,000-follower account gets 800 views a reel, the audience may be inactive or bought.
3. **Audience location and language.** Ask for a screenshot of their audience insights (top cities, countries, age, gender). If you ship only in India and most followers are abroad, move on.
4. **Follower growth pattern.** Sudden jumps of thousands of followers with no viral post to explain them are a warning sign.
5. **Fake follower signs.** Lots of accounts with no profile photo, no posts or random usernames in the follower list; comments that don't match the post.
6. **Past brand work.** Scroll their sponsored posts. Do they disclose properly? Do the posts look forced? Do they promote a competing brand every other week?
7. **Content quality and fit.** Would you be happy to see this person's video on your own page? Tone, lighting and values all matter.
8. **Professionalism.** Do they reply clearly, share rates and timelines, and respect deadlines in the first few messages?

Two or three red flags on one profile is a clear "no".

## Step 3: Write a brief that leaves room for their voice

The fastest way to waste a creator is to send them a script. Their followers know how they talk, and a stiff, read-out ad gets scrolled past.

A good brief fits on one page:

- **Goal:** what you want viewers to do (visit the site, use a code, message on WhatsApp).
- **Key message:** one or two things to get across, in plain words.
- **Must-says and must-not-says:** product name, the code, disclosure; no medical claims, no comparisons with named brands.
- **Format and timing:** one reel and two stories, live between 10 and 15 October.
- **Creative freedom:** "Show how you'd actually use it. Your words, your style."
- **Approval:** whether you want to see a draft before it goes live, and how fast you'll give feedback.

Ask creators to pitch their own idea in two or three lines before filming. Theirs are often better than yours.

## Step 4: Barter or paid?

Barter (free product in return for content) works best with nano creators, for products with real value, and when the creator genuinely wants what you sell. It doesn't work well when the product is cheap, or when you need guaranteed timelines and specific deliverables.

Here's a simple rule of thumb:

- **Barter** for discovery: test lots of nano creators, see who creates naturally.
- **Paid** when you need a fixed date, a defined deliverable, usage rights, or a creator with a proven audience.
- **Barter plus a small fee** is a sensible middle ground for micro creators you want to keep working with.

Barter is still a commercial deal, and it still needs disclosure.

## Step 5: Contracts and usage rights

Even a WhatsApp-level deal should be written down. A one- or two-page agreement covering these points is enough for most small campaigns:

- Deliverables, formats and go-live dates
- Fee or barter value, and when payment happens (many brands pay part upfront and the rest after the post is live)
- Disclosure requirements
- Whether you can review a draft
- How long the post must stay up
- **Usage rights:** can you repost the content on your page, use it in ads, or put it on your website? For how long?
- Exclusivity, if you need it (for example, no competing brands for 30 days)

Usage rights are the part people forget. If you might run a creator's reel as a paid ad, agree that upfront; it's far cheaper to negotiate before the video takes off.

## Step 6: Disclosure and ASCI in plain terms

ASCI (the Advertising Standards Council of India) has guidelines for influencer advertising. In general terms, when there is a material connection between brand and creator, including payment, free product or a family tie, the post should carry a clear, upfront label such as #ad, #collab or #partnership, or use the platform's paid partnership tag.

The label should be easy to see, not buried among 25 hashtags or hidden behind "more". Health, finance and other sensitive categories can carry extra expectations.

<Callout type="warning" title="Disclosure is your problem too">Brands share responsibility for proper disclosure. Put it in the brief and the contract, and check the current ASCI guidelines or take advice, because the rules do get updated.</Callout>

## Step 7: Track with unique codes and UTM links

Likes and views tell you about the content. Codes and links tell you about sales.

- **Unique discount codes** per creator, like PRIYA10 or CHENNAIFOODIE. Shopify and most e-commerce platforms make these easy.
- **UTM links** for the link in bio or story link sticker, with the creator's name in the campaign or content field, so you can see their traffic and orders in Google Analytics 4.
- **A "How did you hear about us?" question** on checkout or in your WhatsApp order flow, to catch customers who saw the reel but typed your name into Google later.

Some people buy days later without the code, so tracked numbers are a floor, not the full picture.

## Step 8: Whitelisting and partnership ads

Once a creator's post performs well organically, you can often boost it. Instagram and Facebook let brands run ads through or alongside a creator's handle (often called partnership ads or whitelisting), with the creator's permission inside the platform.

The creator's name and face tend to feel more trustworthy than a brand ad. Agree this in the contract, set a time limit, and only boost content that has already earned engagement. ## Step 9: Measure cost per acquisition

This is the number that decides who you work with again.

**Cost per acquisition (CPA) = total cost for that creator ÷ number of new customers they brought in.**

Total cost includes the fee, product value at cost, shipping and any ad spend you put behind their post.

Here's an illustrative example. Say Creator A cost ₹8,000 including product and drove 20 orders with her code. Her CPA is ₹400. Creator B cost ₹25,000 and drove 25 orders. His CPA is ₹1,000.

If your average order brings in ₹600 of gross margin, Creator A is profitable on the first order and Creator B isn't, unless those customers come back often. Rebook the A's, drop or renegotiate the B's, and repeat.
